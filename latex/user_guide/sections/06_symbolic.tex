\chapter{Symbolic distillation}
\label{ch:symbolic}

A Fourier Neural Operator reproduces $\rho \mapsto \tau$ to a few percent and
explains nothing. Symbolic distillation searches short algebraic expressions
for one that reproduces the same mapping --- trading accuracy for a formula you
can read, publish, and check against known physics.

\begin{pnote}
Off by default, and an optional install. PySR carries a \textbf{Julia}
toolchain, which it fetches the first time a search runs, so it is not pulled
in by a plain installation.
\end{pnote}

\begin{lstlisting}
pip install -e ".[symbolic]"
poraque-train --config configs/train_config.yaml --task chg2tau --symbolic
\end{lstlisting}

Without the extra, a run with distillation enabled reports the missing
dependency and keeps its trained model rather than failing.

\section{What it can and cannot find}

The features are evaluated \emph{at a point}, so the search space is exactly
the semi-local functionals. Whatever the operator learned that is
\textbf{non-local} cannot be expressed in that space at all, and appears as
irreducible residual.

That makes a poor fit informative rather than disappointing: it puts a number
on how much of the learned map is not semi-local. A near-perfect fit would say
the operator found nothing a GGA-level functional could not have.

\begin{pnote}
Only \opt{chg2tau} is attempted. \opt{ext2chg} is the Hohenberg--Kohn map,
whose whole content is non-local --- a semi-local expression for it would be
meaningless rather than merely inaccurate.
\end{pnote}

\section{The physical variables}

Raw $\rho$ is not enough to build a robust functional. The engine receives the
density together with its \textbf{dimensionless reduced derivatives}, which is
the form a semi-local kinetic functional is actually written in:
%
\begin{equation}
  k_F = (3\pi^2\rho)^{1/3},
  \qquad
  p = \frac{|\nabla\rho|}{2 k_F \rho},
  \qquad
  q = \frac{\nabla^2\rho}{4 k_F^2 \rho}.
\end{equation}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{7.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
Symbol & In the equation & Meaning \\
\midrule
$\rho$ & \opt{rho} & Electron density, atomic units ($e/a_0^3$). \\
$p$    & \opt{p}   & Reduced gradient --- the GGA variable. \\
$q$    & \opt{q}   & Reduced Laplacian --- the meta-GGA variable. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Those names are passed to the engine verbatim, so they are the names that
appear in the result.

Why the reduced forms rather than $|\nabla\rho|$ and $\nabla^2\rho$ directly:
they are dimensionless and invariant under the coordinate scaling
$\rho_\lambda(\rr) = \lambda^3\rho(\lambda\rr)$ that fixes $\Ts$, so a
functional expressed in them holds at every density scale instead of having to
be rediscovered at each. Raw derivatives make every coefficient carry units,
which a genetic search spends its budget approximating.

\subsection{Derivatives are spectral}

$\nabla \to i\GG$ and $\nabla^2 \to -|\GG|^2$, evaluated by FFT. This is
\emph{exact} for the band-limited periodic fields a plane-wave grid carries; a
finite-difference stencil would introduce an error that the search would then
model as physics.

\subsection{Feature schemes}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{5.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
\opt{features} & Variables & Target \\
\midrule
\opt{gga} (default) & \opt{rho}, \opt{p}, \opt{q} & $\tau$ \\
\opt{enhancement}   & \opt{p}, \opt{q}            & $F = \tau/\tauTF$ \\
\opt{raw}           & \opt{rho}, \opt{grad\_rho}, \opt{lap\_rho} & $\tau$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\opt{enhancement} is worth knowing: it is the form the literature writes
kinetic functionals in, so the answer is directly comparable --- Thomas--Fermi
is $F = 1$ and von Weizs\"acker is $F = 5p^2/3$ --- and every constant to be
found is order unity.

\section{Regularization in vacuum}
\label{sec:symbolic-vacuum}

$p$ and $q$ both divide by $\rho$, which decays exponentially into vacuum. Two
things happen, and both are needed:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Every denominator is clamped} at \opt{epsilon}, so $k_F$, $p$
        and $q$ stay finite even where the density underflows.
  \item \textbf{Voxels with $\rho \le$ \opt{epsilon} are dropped.} In vacuum
        $p$ and $q$ are ratios of two vanishing numbers --- noise with a
        plausible magnitude, which corrupts a fit far more quietly than a
        \opt{NaN} would. Vacuum carries no information about the functional, so
        nothing is lost by removing it.
\end{enumerate}

\opt{epsilon} defaults to \opt{1e-8}, in atomic units ($e/a_0^3$). The mask also
removes the slightly negative voxels that band-limiting leaves around the core
peaks (Gibbs ringing), where a fractional power of $\rho$ would be complex.

\begin{ptip}
For a bulk crystal nothing is dropped --- the minimum density is far above the
threshold. It matters for slabs, molecules and anything with real vacuum.
\end{ptip}

\section{Configuration}

\begin{lstlisting}
symbolic:
  enable_symbolic_distillation: false
  target: model          # model | reference
  features: gga          # gga | enhancement | raw
  epsilon: 1.0e-08       # vacuum threshold, e/a0^3
  unary_operations: [exp, log, sqrt, abs]
  binary_operations: ['+', '-', '*', /, ^]
  iterations: 40
  population_size: 33
  populations: 15
  max_depth: 10
  max_size: 30
  parsimony: 0.0032
  n_samples: 4000
  seed: 0
\end{lstlisting}

\opt{target} selects what is fitted. \opt{model} distils the trained operator's
own predictions --- what the network learned, faithful to it including its
errors. \opt{reference} fits the DFT data directly, which is plain symbolic
regression against ground truth and answers a different question.

\opt{unary\_operations} and \opt{binary\_operations} are passed to the engine
untouched. Keep the alphabet small: the search space grows combinatorially, and
an operator that cannot appear in the answer only dilutes the population.
\opt{/} and \opt{log} are the usual sources of singularities on a density
approaching zero.

\opt{n\_samples} caps the rows handed to the search. A single $32^3$ structure
is already $32\,768$ voxels and the cost is linear in them.

\section{Output}

The expression, its accuracy/complexity front and the fit quality are printed
to the terminal, written to the run's JSON summary, and typeset into the PDF
report as display mathematics with $\rho$, $p$ and $q$ rendered properly.

\begin{lstlisting}
  expression   : F = (q * 2.454276) + 0.9163395
  variables    : p, q  [dimensionless (F = tau / tau_TF)]
  complexity   : 5 nodes
  fit          : R2 0.9487   relative L2 0.0736
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{pnote}
Read the front, not just the winner. A single expression hides the trade that
produced it; the front shows what each extra node bought.
\end{pnote}

\begin{pwarn}
The search is stochastic. Two runs with the same \opt{seed} can still differ
unless PySR is also put in deterministic, serial mode --- it warns about this
itself. Treat a single expression as one sample, not as \emph{the} answer.
\end{pwarn}
